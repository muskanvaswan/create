When my family moved back north after 14 years in Bangalore, I thought facing new teachers would be the greatest issue, but instead my issue was facing a new language.

We were a typical North Indian family that had moved from Delhi to Bangalore, because my father was making it in IT. MNCs hit Bangalore like a storm before I had the sense to understand it, and as a result my entire surroundings were kids who spoke English better than their wide variety of mother tongues. I thought I spoke my mother tongues just as well. I topped my Hindi exams and all that. But the changed nuances of a language I had learnt purely from Hindi television were stark as I tried to navigate a new dialect of it altogether.

Eventually it grew on me. My siblings and I stopped conversing in English and incorporated Hindi slang in our vocabulary, and then suddenly I felt closer to all my friends, who confessed they felt pretentious trying to keep up with my running tongue of English. I was suddenly exposed to completely different sides of my friends’ personalities, and even my parents’.

However, I noticed something stranger happen: the more Hindi music I listened to, the more I wanted to wear ethnic outfits, a category of fashion I had found myself averse to growing up. Yet at some point <u>I wanted to hear the word <i>sundar</i> more than I wanted to hear the word <i>beautiful</i>.</u> It awakened a different side of me, a different lens on my world. I found myself searching for specific fabrics to embody a song, I found myself falling for the bamboo pipe folk tunes playing in open markets. I started to romanticize life in a city I used to find rudely “uncivilized”, just by virtue of its language. Simply learning a more casual dialect of a language changed my perception of the world. It would be years before I wondered what the language I had lived beside for 14 years, Kannada, might have shown me.

### A different lens

This ties back into something called the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, the idea that the structure of a language can shape the way you think. This idea is profoundly depicted in a sci-fi movie called \*Arrival\*, in which (spoiler alert) learning a language of loops from an alien world gives the protagonist the ability to view time non-linearly and see the future, past and present at a single point. It made me wonder how many lenses to life I was missing just by not knowing a language.

> **The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis  
> **  
> Named after the linguists Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf. Its strong form, that language fixes what you are able to think, is largely set aside today. Its weaker form, that language nudges what you notice and remember, such as how finely you sort colours or how you picture time, still has experimental support.

### A different self

Before I gave much thought to this, I didn’t truly think losing a language was much more than a loss of cultural identity. After all, my cultural identity had shifted from describing myself as someone born with the incorrect passport to describing myself as “rooted and desi”. I used to chalk it up to growing up, until I moved back to Bangalore. Regardless of my new-found love for Delhi, Bangalore was my home. So I carried my nurtured desi self of suit sets and *jhumkas* back to my corporate job. It lasted a long time, and it still resurfaces, but when I spend my days speaking in English, I end up enjoying English music, and then I end up enjoying dressing modern, and so my stack of suits often sits and stares at me, waiting for me to don the *daftar* aesthetic again. And while I always find joy in my *kurtis* and bangles, even in Bangalore, I prefer them so much more in Delhi.

So <u>my preferences and cultural identity were clearly not a product of just growing up, but rather of the language I spoke most often</u> and the music that romanticized my life.

### Back to Bangalore

Let’s now backtrack a bit to Bangalore. I have been speaking like English is the language of Bangalore, and that’s because to me it is. In all my time in the city, Kannada speakers were in the minority. Surrounded by educated professionals from a variety of places, there was always an unsaid mutual respect in class for all languages. While it was easier to connect with people from your own culture, everyone connected in English and language was not a barrier. Kannada ended up being a minor inconvenience of my day when I had to explain something complex to my maid or ask my school bus conductor to take a different route. It was absent from my Bangalore experience, even more so when I moved back.

### Two itches

Then why even try to learn it? Am I going to preach about not losing vernacular culture, or forcing a language on migrant people just to preserve it? Absolutely not. While English as a functional language makes sense and will likely always stay my primary one, I want to draw attention to the fact that <u>languages are used for more than functional communication.&nbsp;</u> This was a growing realisation that gradually crept up on me as urges, after I realised what languages could influence, but I can split it into two marking points.

The first was a song my school best friend sang. It was in Kannada and she was so emotional while singing it. I could not relate, until I sang a Haryanvi song and saw her unmoved expression. It made me realise that I would never be able to explain the emotion of the song. I am a curious and emotional person, so it made me yearn to understand her language so I could understand her better. This was the first itch.

The second itch came to me in a different nation altogether — South Korea. In this country, like many others, English was not considered the common language of educated people. This is always slightly jarring, since as an Indian you are told that English is the means to your globalization. Most people I met there were kind to a fault, engaging me in conversations and comparisons of culture. I had to pick up at least the word for thank you, so that I could express my respect for their culture as they spoke to me through a layer of Google Translate. They always insisted on saying \*dhanyavaad\* back, even when I explained that most people just say thank you these days. Long conversations with people from different parts of the world made me want to learn more languages, so I could absorb more of the world in the short life that I have, starting from the one I had tried to learn many times already.

### One word a day

Whatever your reason to learn a language, if you have tried doing so as an adult without native speakers around you, you know how hard it is. I have had my friends explain some words to me quite often, but it never lasted. I only remember some broken words. Then I thought, what if I knew enough broken words to piece together a sentence, and remembered most of them long enough? But something like Duolingo is intense and time-consuming. ==I didn’t want to be perfect at the language, I wanted to understand it just enough.== So I could understand the world and my loved ones better and show up for them. So I am not an alien while walking through a market, so shop owners may warm to me while I negotiate, so my fashion sense may require a \*pattu pavade\*.

This was the idea behind the language calendar (themeridianpress.in/shop): one word every day. Even if you retain only 25% of the 365 days, that’s about 90 words, enough to feel like you are a part of the conversation. Maybe even enough to one day hear my best friend sing that song and feel a little of what she feels.

I want to add to this all the languages spoken by my friends and loved ones, and so there will be more languages covered here soon, starting with a Telugu version.
